The Art of the In-Between: Discovering the Magic of the Miles Between National Parks

2026-06-16 · National Parks · Tags: roadtripamerica, The, national parks

We plan for months. We map out every trail, every overlook, every iconic vista within the park boundaries. We reserve campsites, book timed entry passes, and pack our gear with military precision. But here’s the truth we often forget: the national park is only part of the story. The road that carries you there—and the miles that stretch between one park and the next—are where the real adventure often hides. As a Road Warrior who has logged tens of thousands of miles chasing the nation’s natural wonders, I’ve learned that the most memorable moments rarely happen inside the gates. They happen in the dusty crossroads, the forgotten diners, the winding backroads that connect our greatest treasures. This is the art of the in-between—and it might just transform the way you plan your next national park road trip.

Why the In-Between Matters More Than You Think

When we dream of a national park road trip, we picture the Grand Canyon at sunrise, the geysers of Yellowstone, the granite spires of Yosemite. We mentally skip over the 400 miles of high desert, prairie, or forest that separate these wonders. But those miles are not empty. They are a tapestry of geological history, human stories, and quiet beauty that the park’s crowded overlooks can never replicate. The in-between is where you find the unexpected—a roadside ghost town, a canyon carved by a river you’ve never heard of, a field of wildflowers that stops you in your tracks. These are the places that don’t make the postcards, but they’re the ones you’ll talk about for years.

Moreover, the in-between offers a crucial counterbalance to the intensity of park visits. National parks, especially the famous ones, are often crowded, demanding, and physically exhausting. The drive between them gives you time to decompress, to process what you’ve seen, and to build anticipation for what’s ahead. It’s a rhythm—the crescendo of the park, the quiet interlude of the road, the next crescendo. Without the interlude, the experience becomes a blur. By embracing the in-between, you turn your road trip from a checklist of destinations into a continuous journey of discovery.

Planning for the Unexpected: Researching the Corridors

You don’t need to abandon your itinerary to embrace the in-between. Instead, you need to shift your planning focus. When you map your route from one park to another, spend as much time researching the corridor as you do the parks themselves. Look for scenic byways, state parks, national monuments, and historic sites that lie just off your main route. A simple Google Maps search for “attractions near [highway]” can reveal hidden gems, but for deeper discovery, use tools like the National Park Service’s “Find a Park” map, or consult regional travel guides and local tourism websites. I’ve found that the best in-between spots are often small, quirky, and under-marketed—like the world’s largest ball of twine in Cawker City, Kansas, or the Painted Desert’s lesser-known overlooks along Route 89 in Arizona.

Another tactic is to plan your driving days with built-in flexibility. Instead of aiming for a specific campsite each night, leave a buffer of 50 to 100 miles. That way, when you spot a sign for a “historic landmark” or a “scenic overlook” that you didn’t know about, you can pull over without wrecking your schedule. And don’t be afraid to take the slower, more scenic route. The interstates are efficient, but they’re also sterile. The two-lane highways that parallel them often follow old alignments, passing through small towns and offering views that the interstate’s sound walls and rest stops hide. A little extra driving time is a small price for a richer experience.

Small Towns and Local Flavors: The Heartbeat of the Road

Between the great parks, you’ll find America’s small towns—places that live and breathe by the road. These towns are not just gas stations and fast food; they are repositories of local culture, history, and cuisine. Stop for lunch at a diner that’s been serving travelers since 1935. Chat with the owner, who’ll tell you about the town’s boom-and-bust cycles. Visit the local museum, often housed in an old courthouse or depot, and learn about the people who settled this land long before the national parks were established. These interactions are the antidote to the anonymity of the highway.

Food is a particularly powerful way to connect with the in-between. Skip the chain restaurants and look for places with hand-painted signs and crowded parking lots. In the Southwest, that might be a family-run tamale shop; in the Rockies, a bison burger joint; in the Pacific Northwest, a roadside stand selling huckleberry pies. Not only will you eat better, but you’ll also support local economies that often rely on travelers. And don’t forget the farmers’ markets—they’re a goldmine for fresh produce, homemade jams, and regional specialties that make excellent picnic supplies for your next park visit. I always pack a small cooler and a stash of reusable containers, so I can stock up on local goodies as I go.

Scenic Byways and Overlooks: The Road as Destination

Some of the most breathtaking landscapes in America are not inside national parks—they’re the corridors that connect them. The Beartooth Highway between Yellowstone and Billings, Montana, is a prime example. It climbs to over 10,000 feet, offering alpine vistas that rival any park. Similarly, the Million Dollar Highway in Colorado, the Going-to-the-Sun Road in Glacier (okay, that’s in the park, but the approach roads are just as stunning), and the Pacific Coast Highway are all journeys in their own right. When you plan your route, identify these scenic byways and make them the backbone of your drive. Even if they add hours, the payoff is immense.

Don’t overlook the simple pleasure of pulling over. On any given stretch of two-lane road, there will be pullouts, viewpoints, and interpretive signs. Use them. Stretch your legs, take a deep breath, and actually look at the landscape. I’ve discovered some of my favorite spots this way—a hidden waterfall in the Cascades, a field of petrified wood in Arizona, a prairie dog town in South Dakota. These moments of serendipity are the soul of the in-between. They remind us that the journey is not just a means to an end, but an end in itself.

Making Time for Spontaneity: The Art of the Unplanned Detour

The best in-between experiences are often the ones you never planned for. That’s why it’s crucial to build spontaneity into your road trip. This doesn’t mean throwing your itinerary out the window; it means leaving gaps for the unexpected. When you see a sign for a “natural arch” or a “historic fort” that you’ve never heard of, allow yourself to take the detour—even if it adds an hour to your drive. The worst-case scenario is that you see something interesting; the best-case is that you stumble upon a place that becomes the highlight of your trip.

To make spontaneity easier, adopt a few habits. First, keep your gas tank at least half full at all times, so you never have to skip a detour because of fuel anxiety. Second, carry a physical map—yes, the old-fashioned paper kind—so you can see the network of roads that the GPS might ignore. Third, set a daily rule: no more than six hours of driving, with at least one unplanned stop. This forces you to slow down and engage with the world around you. Finally, talk to locals. At a gas station or a café, ask, “If you had an extra hour, where would you go?” The answers will surprise you.

Balancing Time: How to Avoid the Rush and Savor the Miles

One of the biggest mistakes road trippers make is trying to see too much in too little time. They cram four parks into five days, spending most of their time behind the wheel and barely scratching the surface. The in-between suffers most in this scenario. To truly embrace the art of the in-between, you need to reimagine your trip’s pace. Instead of asking, “How many parks can I check off?” ask, “How much can I experience along the way?” That might mean visiting fewer parks, but spending more time in the corridors that connect them. For example, instead of a whirlwind tour of Utah’s Mighty Five, pick three parks and add a day for the dramatic Highway 12 through Grand Staircase-Escalante, or the backroads of the San Rafael Swell.

This approach also reduces stress. When you’re not racing to the next park, you can linger over a sunset, take a spontaneous hike, or simply sit in a town square and watch the world go by. You’ll return home feeling refreshed, not exhausted. And you’ll have stories that go beyond “I saw the Grand Canyon”—stories about the old miner who told you where to find a fossil bed, the diner waitress who recommended a hidden hot spring, the moment you saw a herd of pronghorn galloping across the prairie. These are the memories that last.

Conclusion

America’s national parks are the crown jewels of our landscape, but the roads that connect them are the threads that bind the crown together. The art of the in-between is about shifting your perspective—from seeing the drive as a necessary evil to seeing it as an integral part of the adventure. By researching the corridors, embracing small towns, seeking out scenic byways, and leaving room for spontaneity, you’ll discover that the miles between the gates are as rich and rewarding as the parks themselves. So next time you plan a national park road trip, don’t just plot your points on a map. Give the in-between the attention it deserves. You’ll come home with more than a passport full of stamps—you’ll come home with a heart full of stories. Safe travels, Road Warriors.
